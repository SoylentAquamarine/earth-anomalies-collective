# Prior-claims catalog

Previously publicized "Google Earth/satellite imagery anomaly" claims and their documented resolution
status — so this project doesn't re-litigate already-examined cases, and a reader can check "has this
specific claim already been looked at" before proposing it again. See
`logs/2026-10-09-sq3-prior-claims-catalog-first-pass.md` for method; this is a first pass, not yet
exhaustive.

## The Canary Islands "Atlantis grid" (2009)

**Claim:** in February 2009, the UK tabloid *The Sun* reported that a grid of straight, crisscrossing
lines on the seafloor, roughly the size of Wales, spotted by British aeronautical engineer Bernie Bamford
using Google Ocean, "could show" Atlantis — located roughly 600–620 miles west of the Canary Islands, in
the Madeira Abyssal Plain.

**Resolution — Explained.** Directly fetched (Scientific American's news blog): Google's own official
explanation is that the pattern is "an artifact of the data collection process" — bathymetric data is
typically gathered by boats using sonar, and the grid lines trace the boats' survey paths; the gaps
between lines reflect how little of the deep ocean floor has been mapped at all. A geoscience blog (not
directly fetched this pass) makes the same point in more technical terms, and notes this kind of
sonar-survey-track artifact appears "just about anywhere" in Google's bathymetry data — this instance is
just more visually striking than most.

**Evidentiary tier:** directly fetched for the official explanation and the tabloid-origin framing; the
technical geoscience-blog corroboration is search-synthesis tier only, not independently re-fetched this
pass.

## The Richat Structure / "Eye of the Sahara" (Mauritania)

**Claim:** a roughly 40 km-wide, concentric-ring geological formation in the Mauritanian Sahara,
frequently linked in popular claims to Plato's Atlantis, on the basis of its ring shape.

**Resolution — Explained (geology), contested (Atlantis link).** The structure's geology is well
established in the literature (search-synthesis tier this pass, direct fetch of the Britannica source
blocked with HTTP 403): originally hypothesized as a meteor-impact crater, now understood as a deeply
eroded geologic dome, with a subsurface alkaline igneous intrusion that deformed and displaced the rock
layers above it as it uplifted — composed of gabbroic ring dikes and extrusive rhyolitic centers, dated
to roughly 98–99 million years ago (mid-Cretaceous). The Atlantis-link claims have specific, named
counter-objections: the ring-shape match is not as precise as claimed; mainstream classicists treat
Plato's Atlantis as a deliberate philosophical device, not a historical report; and a commonly repeated
claim that the structure "wasn't even discovered until the space age" is itself a mischaracterization —
it was used as a landmark by WWII-era pilots and first scientifically described in 1948, well before
satellite imagery existed (satellite imagery made it *famous*, not *discovered*).

**Evidentiary tier:** search-synthesis only this pass — not yet upgraded to a direct primary-source read.
Flagged as a next-cycle target (a geology paper, e.g. Liu et al., was referenced as a likely primary
source but not itself fetched).

## The Kazakh Steppe Geoglyphs (Turgai region, northern Kazakhstan)

**Claim:** a set of at least 260 large-scale earthworks (mounds, trenches, ramparts in roughly five basic
shapes), first noticed by Dmitriy Dey using Google Earth in 2007.

**Status — genuinely open, not a clean "confirmed" case.** This project's own bootstrap scaffolding
(written before any research) described this as "a genuine, confirmed set of ancient earthworks... a
real anomaly-turned-archaeological-finding." **That framing is corrected here, not silently carried
forward**: directly fetched National Geographic coverage establishes the actual state of evidence is
considerably more provisional. Dey, the finder, is a business manager and satellite-imagery enthusiast,
not a professional archaeologist. Age is only partially confirmed: optically stimulated luminescence
(OSL) dating by an actual excavation team (Matuzeviciute et al.) places two of the 55+ known sites at
800 BC and 750 BC specifically ("accurate within 20 years," per the fetched article) — but Dey's own,
much older estimate of up to 8,000 years (based on erosion rates and nearby Neolithic flints) is disputed
by the same excavating team, who argue the flints could be later intrusions and that erosion-rate dating
is unreliable. As of the fetched article, the dating findings were **not yet peer-reviewed**. One outside
archaeologist, Michael Frachetti (not involved in the research), is quoted calling a related
interpretive claim (a "sun cult" purpose) "not necessarily wrong" but adding "it just doesn't qualify as
science" in its current form.

**Evidentiary tier:** directly fetched (National Geographic), a real upgrade from the bootstrap
scaffolding's unverified assertion.

**Why this case is still useful as a calibration reference** — just not for the reason originally
assumed: it's a good illustration of this project's own evidence ladder in action on a real,
still-developing case (real physical features, partial and contested dating, pre-peer-review status),
rather than a settled "anomaly confirmed" example.
